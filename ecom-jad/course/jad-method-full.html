01 · Responsible use

Read this first

This guide explains a way of working. It does not promise a result, and no part of it should be read as a claim about money you will make. What you earn — if you earn anything — depends on the product you build, the market you choose, the budget you can afford to lose, and how carefully you execute. That is not a disclaimer bolted on at the end. It is the honest frame for everything that follows.

What this guide will not do

It will not give you a product idea that is guaranteed to sell, a number to expect, or a shortcut past the platforms' rules. Anyone selling you those three things is selling you a story.

You are running a business, not a side quest

Selling a digital product means taking money from real people, in a real jurisdiction, under real consumer law. From your first sale you are responsible for accurate descriptions, working delivery, honest advertising, correct tax treatment and a way for buyers to reach you. The guide covers all of it, but it cannot know where you live.

Rules differ by country

Tax on digital goods, the right to cancel a purchase, what counts as a valid review, and how you must handle personal data are not the same everywhere. Wherever a rule depends on your market, this guide names the question and tells you to check it — instead of giving you an answer that is only true in one country.

Facts go stale

Prices, plan names, menu paths and advertising policies change, sometimes monthly. Every place in this guide where that matters carries a date stamp like the one below, and names the official page to check. If the date is old, trust the official page, not this one.

ECOM JADWhat the method is

02 · What the method is

The whole model in one paragraph

You find a narrow problem that a specific group of people actively wants solved. You check that they exist, that they already pay to solve it, and that you can build something genuinely useful. You make the smallest version worth paying for, deliver it as a file, and put it behind a simple store that takes payment and sends the file automatically. Then you buy a small, measured amount of traffic and read what happens — not the headline number, the whole funnel. Nothing is warehoused, packed or shipped at any point.

02 · What the method is

What it is not

A digital product costs almost nothing to copy. That single fact gets stretched into a claim it cannot carry — that the whole business is nearly free. Copying is cheap. Everything else costs something.

The claim you have heard
  • No costs, because there is no stock
  • Set it up once, earn while you sleep
  • Anyone can sell anything
  • Ads turn spending into earning
What is actually true
  • Platform fees, payment fees, tools, refunds and ads are all real
  • Support, updates and answering buyers continue for as long as you sell
  • You need to know something the buyer does not
  • Ads test demand; they do not create it

What the method actually removes

Compared with selling a physical product, three specific risks disappear: money tied up in stock you have not sold, a supplier whose mistakes become your refunds, and delivery times you cannot control. Those are the three that most often end a beginner's first store. Removing them is a genuine advantage — and it is a different claim from "no risk".

The cost that replaces them

You now carry the quality risk yourself. Nobody else made the product, so nobody else can be blamed if it is thin. Module 07 is about making it not thin.

03 · Idea library

How to read this list

None of what follows is evidence. An idea appearing in a list does not mean people are buying it, that the market has room, or that you are the right person to build it. The list exists so you have somewhere to start looking, and so you notice categories you would not have thought of.

Read it with one question in mind: which of these do I already understand well enough to explain to someone else? That is a far better filter than which one sounds most profitable, and it is the filter Module 05 scores you on.

Three areas carry extra risk

Health and the body. Money and income. Anyone's appearance. In these areas the advertising platforms restrict what you may say, consumer law is stricter about claims, and a wrong sentence is not merely a policy problem — it can mislead someone about their own health or finances. You can work in them, but only with real knowledge, careful wording, and claims you could defend.

03 · Idea library

A note on trends

Copying whatever is selling this month is the most common way beginners choose, and the worst. By the time a trend is visible enough for you to copy, the audience has already seen four versions of it, and you are competing on price against people who got there first. A narrower problem you genuinely understand beats a broad one you read about last week.

What "narrow" means

Not "fitness". Not "fitness for beginners". Something closer to: "the first four weeks in a gym, for someone who has never used free weights and does not want to ask." Narrow enough that the buyer recognises themselves in one sentence.

04 · Choosing a problem and an audience

Problem first, format second

Most people start in the wrong place. They decide "I want to sell a PDF", then look for something to put in it. That order produces products nobody asked for, because the format was chosen before anyone knew what job it had to do.

Start instead with a sentence in this shape: this specific group needs an easier route from where they are now to where they want to be. Once that sentence is true and specific, the format is usually obvious.

Starting from the format
  • "A productivity guide"
  • Audience: anyone busy
  • You write what you can think of
  • The ad has nothing specific to say
Starting from the problem
  • "Freelancers who miss invoices when a project ends"
  • Audience: one recognisable person
  • You write what solves that
  • The ad writes itself

Build on what you actually know

A product grounded in your own experience, or in sources you can point to, is both safer and worth more than a repackaged trend. Safer, because you will not accidentally state something false. Worth more, because you can answer the questions a buyer asks afterwards — and those questions are where thin products fall apart.

A useful test

Could you talk about this for twenty minutes to someone who is stuck, without preparing? If not, you do not know it well enough to charge for it yet. That is not a permanent no — it is a reading list.

04 · Choosing a problem and an audience

Research without copying

The point of research is to learn the words your audience uses and the objections they raise — not to collect material. Everything you produce must be your own.

Collect the questions people actually ask. Search suggestions, forum threads, comment sections, reviews of existing products, and competitors' public descriptions. Write down questions, not answers.
Sort what you found into four piles: problems, wants, objections, and the exact words the audience uses for each. The fourth pile is the one most people skip and the one your ad copy comes from.
Find the gaps. What existing answers are too general, too advanced, too expensive, or missing altogether? A gap is your opening; an already-crowded answer is not.
Build your own structure and your own examples. Never copy text, layout, worksheets, video or anyone's customer material — not as a starting point, not "just for now".
Check the rights on everything you use. Trademarks, images, music, fonts and template licences all have terms, and "found online" is not one of them.
Why copying ends the business, not just the product

Copied material can get your store taken down, your ad account closed and your payment provider to withhold funds — usually all at once, and usually after you have spent money on traffic. It is also the one mistake you cannot quietly fix later, because the buyer already has the file.

The minimum before you spend anything on ads

None of this requires a finished product, and all of it is cheaper than finding out from a failed campaign.

  • Five real conversations, or structured answers, from people in the audience — where that is possible.

ECOM JADValidation and the scorecard

05 · Validation and the scorecard

What validation actually is

It is not asking whether people like your idea. Everyone likes ideas. It is looking for evidence that a specific group already has this problem, already spends time or money trying to solve it, and is not already well served.

Notice that all three of those are things you can go and check today, before you write a page of product. That is the entire point. A weak idea caught now costs you an afternoon; caught after you have built the product, bought a domain and run a campaign, it costs you the whole budget and a month.

Not validation
  • Friends saying it sounds good
  • A large market existing
  • Someone else selling something similar
  • Your own certainty
Validation
  • People describing this problem in their own words
  • Existing paid products, with buyers
  • Repeated questions nobody answers well
  • Someone in the audience saying what they would pay
The trap in "someone is already selling it"

Competition is evidence of demand, which is good. It is not evidence that there is room for you, which is different. What you are looking for is a competitor doing well and a group of buyers whose specific version of the problem that competitor does not address.

06 · The offer, and honest claims

An idea is not an offer

An idea is "a budget template". An offer says who it is for, what changes for them, how, and what it costs them in effort. Until you can write that in one sentence, you have nothing to put on a product page and nothing for an ad to say.

The formula

Offer sentence
For [a specific audience]
who struggle with [a concrete problem],
[product name] helps them [a realistic outcome]
through [the mechanism, or what it simplifies],
without [the main objection],
provided they [the effort they must put in].

The last clause is the one people leave out, and it is the one that keeps the sentence honest. Every real product requires something of the buyer. Saying so does not weaken the offer — it filters out the people who would have asked for a refund.

Worked through

For freelancers who lose track of unbilled work when a project ends, the Handover Kit gives them a single closing checklist and an invoice template, without needing new software, provided they run it at the end of every project.

Notice what the sentence does not promise: more clients, more income, or a specific amount of time saved. It promises a structure. That is a claim you can defend.

Test it against a stranger

Read the sentence to someone outside the audience. If they can tell you who it is for and what changes, it works. If they ask "so what does it actually do?", the mechanism clause is vague — and vague mechanisms are where refunds come from.

06 · The offer, and honest claims

Naming and positioning

A name should describe the outcome, the audience or the method. It should not promise a result you cannot control.

Names that work
  • Describe what it is: "The Handover Kit"
  • Name the audience: "First Four Weeks"
  • Name the method: "One-Page Budget"
Names that create problems
  • Imply treatment or a cure
  • Imply guaranteed money or returns
  • Imply speed you cannot control
  • Use someone else's trademark

Write the page before you design anything

Before you open a design tool, write these five things as plain text. If they are weak, no amount of design fixes them — and if they are strong, the design job becomes obvious.

A headline naming the outcome and the audience.
A subheadline naming the format, so nobody is surprised by what arrives.
Three benefit lines — understand X, do Y, keep track of Z.
A concrete "what's included": the actual files, sections, templates and counts.
A "who this is not for" list. It costs you a few buyers and saves you every refund those buyers would have asked for.
Version 1.0, not version 3

Build the smallest complete thing that delivers the outcome. Bonuses, extra modules and a template library come after demand is proven — not before. Most first products die of scope, not of quality.

Claims you cannot make

This is not only about advertising policy. Misleading claims are regulated in most markets, and the rules apply to your product page, your ads and your emails alike. Where the specifics differ by country, the direction never does: say what you can evidence, and nothing more.

"Guaranteed", "risk-free", "passive"Say what the buyer has to do for it to work

Fabricated reviews are prohibited outright across the EU and restricted in most other markets, and platforms remove stores for them. If a reviewer received the product free, that must be visible — a free copy is not a purchase, and presenting it as one is the same offence in a smaller font.

  • Write the five page elements as plain text, before any design.

ECOM JADBuilding version 1.0

07 · Building version 1.0

Start from one outcome, not a table of contents

Before you plan any chapters, write a single sentence: after using this, the buyer can do, understand or organise X. One outcome. If you need "and", you have two products, and the first one is not finished.

Write the single outcome. Specific enough that you could tell whether someone achieved it.
List the sub-problems that stand between the buyer and that outcome, in the order they hit them — not in the order that is tidy to write.
Delete everything that does not serve the outcome. This is the step that decides whether the product is good. Interesting material that does not move the buyer forward is padding, and buyers can tell.
Give each remaining piece the same shape: where you are now, what to do, a worked example, something to fill in, and a check that it worked.
Choose the format per piece. Reference material wants a document; calculation wants a spreadsheet; something reused wants a template; something you cannot picture from text wants a recording.
Finish the core before you add anything. No bonuses, no extra modules, no library — until version 1.0 works end to end.
The most common failure

Not bad writing. Scope. A first product that tries to cover everything takes three months, never ships, and would have been worse than the narrow one you could have finished in a week and improved with real feedback.

07 · Building version 1.0

Rules while you produce

The text

  • All core content is your own and fact-checked. An AI draft is a starting point, never a shipped page — you are the one who is accountable for every sentence in it.
  • Keep terminology, heading levels, typography and image style consistent. Inconsistency reads as carelessness, and carelessness reads as "this was made quickly".
  • Make instructions testable: the reader must be able to compare what they got with what they should have got.

The files

  • Compress images to a sensible file size, but never past the point where text in them is readable.
  • Add alt text or descriptive captions in the editable source, so the material works for more people and survives a format change.
  • Keep working files separate from the delivery file. The buyer gets one clean file, not your folder.
Before any screenshot goes in

Remove or mask API keys, tokens, email addresses, customer names, order numbers, store identifiers and payment details. A screenshot you took in a hurry is the most common way private data ends up in a file you then sell to strangers.

Rights

Write down the licence for every external image, icon, font, music file and template you use, and keep that list with the working files. "I found it online" is not a licence, and the question always arrives after the product is selling.

07 · Building version 1.0

Quality control, before it goes on sale

Run all of it. Each step catches a different class of problem, and the cheapest one to skip is always the one that would have caught the real fault.

Fact-check every claim and every link. Anything time-sensitive gets a date and a source.
Have one person from the audience follow it with no help from you. Watch where they hesitate. Do not explain — write down the hesitation.
Fix what they stumbled on: ambiguous steps, missing prerequisites, broken links, an example that assumed knowledge you never gave them.

08 · Pricing, costs and break-even

The price is not what you keep

A digital product costs nothing to copy. That is true, and it is where most people stop thinking. Copying is free; the order is not. Between the price on the page and the money in your account sit five deductions, and every one of them is real.

Tax is the one to check first

Payment processing is usually a percentage plus a fixed amount. At a high price the fixed part disappears into the percentage. At a low price it can be the largest single deduction. Break-even cost per purchase

This is the number to write on a sticky note. It is the most an order may cost you in advertising before that order stops making money.

The three formulas
Net per order      = price - tax - payment fees - refund reserve

Break-even CPA     = net per order - fixed cost carried per order

Profit per order   = net per order - fixed cost per order - actual CPA

Once you have the middle number, every advertising decision becomes simple. Cost per purchase below it, with room to spare: the campaign is working. Above it: the campaign is losing money, no matter what the platform's dashboard says about return on ad spend.

Setting the price

Price from what the outcome is worth to the buyer and what their alternatives cost — not from the monthly figure you would like to reach. Working backwards from a desired income produces a price the market never agreed to.

Reasons that are not reasons
  • "I want to earn X per month"
  • "It took me a long time to make"
  • "Everyone else charges this"
  • "Cheap will sell more"
Reasons that are
  • What the alternative costs in money or hours
  • How specific the outcome is
  • How much of the work you have already done for them
  • What break-even leaves room for

Both are viable; they demand different things. A low price needs volume, and volume needs cheap traffic — which means the fixed processing fee and the break-even CPA become the whole business. Cost control comes first, not last

Generated video is the most expensive thing in this course, and the cost arrives per attempt rather than per finished clip. A vague idea and a generous credit balance is how people spend a month's ad budget on footage they never use.

What stays fixed while the test runs

  • The same product, price and landing page while creatives are compared.
  • The same objective and the same conversion event.
  • The same market and broadly similar delivery conditions.
  • One main difference per creative test.
  • No significant store edits mid-test without writing them in the log.

Stop rules

  • Pause immediately on a broken checkout, a wrong price, a failed delivery or a duplicated purchase event.

  • Never exceed the maximum loss you decided before starting.
  • Do not judge an ad on a handful of impressions. Define in advance what counts as enough data for your budget and traffic.
  • If traffic is relevant but nobody interacts with the product, investigate the offer and the page before spending more.

Why return on ad spend can mislead

It uses the revenue the platform could attribute to itself, before tax, fees and refunds. A campaign can show a healthy return and still lose money on every order. Cost per purchase measured against your break-even figure from Module 08 is the honest test.

Compare the same thing in more than one place

  • The ad platform — delivery, clicks and attributed conversions.
  • Your store — real orders, net revenue, discounts and refunds.
  • Web analytics — sessions, landing page, device and source.
  • The payment provider — completed and declined payments.
  • The delivery app — emails sent, links clicked, files downloaded.

These will not agree, because of attribution, consent, time zones and blocked tracking. Document the difference rather than choosing the flattering number.

How to scale without breaking it

Increase budget in small controlled steps, and watch cost per purchase after each one.
Do not duplicate campaigns without a stated hypothesis for why the copy will behave differently.
Widen the audience or the keywords gradually, not all at once.
Reuse the winning angle in new formats before abandoning it for a new angle.
Build retargeting only once the audience has meaningful volume and consent allows it.
